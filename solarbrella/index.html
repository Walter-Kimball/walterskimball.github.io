This device resulted from NYU Tandon's EG-1004 semester-long design project, where our team was tasked with developing a solution to a specific engineering problem. Operating under our company name, WitTech Junction, I served as Team Lead and Head of Design, managing communications with professors and project mentors while generating the vast majority of the team's CAD models and engineering drawings. My team members included Justin Oh, a computer engineer who led system programming and electrical wiring, and Tony Guan, a mechanical engineer who managed project logistics, presentation materials, and the construction of non-design components.

Structured as a mock industry project, progress was evaluated across three major milestones. For Milestone 1, we established our core product concept—the Solarbrella—conducted market research on existing alternatives, completed initial material sourcing, and established cost projections alongside a formal teamwork agreement. At this early stage, the project remained in a conceptual phase while staying fully on schedule and within budget.

By Milestone 2, we progressed through two additional design iterations. I modified the solar panel array actuation geometry to optimize rotational torque delivery for our servo motors. Simultaneously, the team finalized component selections, purchasing solar panels, a lead-acid storage battery, and a solar charge controller to regulate battery charging. Although budget targets were met, we temporarily fell behind schedule on constructing the physical device and its specialized mounting sunshade.

By Milestone 3, we recovered our schedule and finalized the Solarbrella V6 design and mounting assembly within all technical specifications and budget limits. Version 6 featured an active solar-tracking array governed by four photoresistors arranged in a quadrant box separated by divider walls. When direct sunlight shifted, the divider walls cast shadows onto specific sensors, triggering servo routines to rotate or tilt the array into direct alignment. Across the final three design cycles, we achieved a 34% total weight reduction and centered the overall mass within the shade structure, eliminating weight imbalances that could cause mechanical oscillation during active rotation.

On Commission Day, our team completed testing five days ahead of schedule. We utilized this buffer time to polish our final presentation, earning extra credit. NYU EG-1004 faculty selected the Solarbrella to be preserved as a permanent benchmark demonstration model for future engineering classes.
