This device resulted from my NYU Tandon EG-1004 class's semester-long design project where we were tasked with creating a device for solving a specific problem. My group consisted of two other members, one being another mechanical engineer and the other a computer engineer. I took a team lead position and handled the communicating for the group with our professors and group mentor. I was also the head of design for the team, creating the vast majority of the drawings and CAD for the project. The computer engineer was the head of programming and handled the wiring and coding. Lastly, the other mechanical engineer managed the logistics for presentations and served as head of production, overseeing appearance and construction of non-design components.

This was a mock industry project structured around three key milestones. For our first milestone, we agreed on the initial design for the Solarbrella, completed preliminary market research on competing products, and identified required materials. This presentation included cost predictions and a teamwork agreement outlining individual responsibilities. We established our company name as WitTech Junction and named the product Solarbrella. At this stage, we were in a conceptual phase while staying on budget and schedule.

By milestone two, we progressed through two additional design iterations. I refined how the solar panel arrays actuated to optimize their rotation via servo motors. We also finalized purchasing decisions for core hardware, including a solar charge controller to regulate battery power, solar panels, and a lead-acid battery. For this benchmark, we maintained our budget while accelerating physical construction of the custom sunshade mount.

By milestone three, we returned fully to schedule with a finalized design (V6) and a fully assembled umbrella housing ready for mounting. Budget goals were achieved and all technical specifications were satisfied.

V6 represented significant optimizations over V1. The solar tracking array was governed by four photoresistors arranged in a quadrant configuration separated by barrier walls. The system evaluated ambient light readings to calculate optimal positioning, actively rotating or tilting the panels toward direct sunlight whenever shadows fell across the directional sensors.

We also achieved major structural improvements. Across three development cycles, we achieved a 34% total weight reduction while centering the overall mass within the shade structure. Balancing the center of gravity within a constrained envelope prevented mechanical oscillation during active servo rotation, ensuring rotational stability and operational safety.

On commission day, our team completed testing five days ahead of schedule. We utilized this buffer to finalize our documentation and formal presentation, earning extra credit. NYU EG-1004 faculty selected the Solarbrella to be preserved as a permanent benchmark demonstration project for future engineering classes.
