Howell Labs 7600 Sea Water Chlorinator

Internship Assignment

Assigned Project

Autodesk Inventor Autodesk Vault Engineering Drawings BOM Epicor ERP GD&T Weldments Weld Callouts Sheet Metal Drawings Smartsheet

During my engineering internship at Howell Laboratories, the design team was actively modernizing their flagship 7600 Sea Water Chlorinator to replace legacy, unsourceable components. Because this critical machinery was designated for upcoming U.S. Navy vessels, updating the design package was treated as a top company priority. Near the conclusion of the core design phase, a substantial number of production drawings were required for manufacturing review, professional engineering stamping, and U.S. Navy approval.

Fellow intern Gavin Pardon and I were tasked with drafting component, sheet metal, and assembly drawings for the updated 7600 system, assigning new part numbers and logging all files into Autodesk Vault. This work strictly adhered to formal ASME Y14.5 GD&T standards, internal Howell drawing conventions, and military manufacturing notes. My responsibilities focused on weldments, sheet metal bending, sub-assemblies, and surface finishing/powder coating drawing packages. Each drawing underwent a rigorous redline revision process—first passing review by licensed professional engineers, followed by manufacturing shop floor feedback, before final stamping and Navy authorization.

Once approved, we entered each component into Epicor ERP to establish manufacturing routings. This involved defining part entries and building operational steps—such as material cutting, machining, welding, and assembly—along with estimated work center hours. By handling this comprehensive drafting and ERP release process, our assistance reduced project lead time by approximately 30%, saving nearly a full month on a 2 to 3-month schedule.

This success prompted Howell Laboratories to create a permanent full-time drafting role to maintain the accelerated workflow established during our internship. Returning to Howell in December 2024, I had the opportunity to see the 7600 enter physical production and ship for official U.S. Navy qualification testing. The project provided deep practical insight into naval equipment requirements, class-based specifications, and formal drawing release workflows.

During my Summer 2024 internship at Howell Laboratories, I was assigned to digitize and model Howell's long-standing 4870 Air Control Panel. The system was actively manufactured and reviewed using legacy hand-sketched drawings. Because the engineering team planned to overhaul the 4870 within a few months, the lack of a 3D CAD model presented a significant bottleneck for client presentations and design reviews.

My objective was to audit the entire Bill of Materials (BOM), model every individual component, and construct a complete top-level assembly along with production drawing documentation. Early in the process, I researched supplier databases for commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) CAD models and discovered that several key components—including custom pneumatic fittings, pressure gauges, and regulators—were going out of production and predated 3D digital records. To resolve this, I measured physical stock in the warehouse by hand to reverse-engineer exact 3D models, while concurrently building in-house part CAD from legacy 2D drawings.

Once all constituent models were finalized, I constructed the top-level 4870 assembly in Autodesk Inventor. Custom metadata was assigned to every part file so that importing the assembly into Autodesk Vault generated a fully formatted, automated BOM structure. I then authored the final production drawing set containing complete weld callouts, BOM tables, quality control dimensions, and shop manufacturing notes.

Delivering this digital CAD package and identifying obsolete legacy components eliminated half of the preliminary legwork required for the upcoming 4870 overhaul, while allowing Howell to initiate early supplier outreach for drop-in replacement hardware.

This device resulted from my NYU Tandon EG-1004 class's semester-long design project where we were tasked with creating a device for solving a specific problem. My group consisted of two other members, one being another mechanical engineer and the other a computer engineer. I took a team lead position and handled the communicating for the group with our professors and group mentor. I was also the head of design for the team, creating the vast majority of the drawings and CAD for the project. The computer engineer was the head of programming and handled the wiring and coding. Lastly, the other mechanical engineer managed the logistics for presentations and served as head of production, overseeing appearance and construction of non-design components.

This was a mock industry project structured around three key milestones. For our first milestone, we agreed on the initial design for the Solarbrella, completed preliminary market research on competing products, and identified required materials. This presentation included cost predictions and a teamwork agreement outlining individual responsibilities. We established our company name as WitTech Junction and named the product Solarbrella. At this stage, we were in a conceptual phase while staying on budget and schedule.

By milestone two, we progressed through two additional design iterations. I refined how the solar panel arrays actuated to optimize their rotation via servo motors. We also finalized purchasing decisions for core hardware, including a solar charge controller to regulate battery power, solar panels, and a lead-acid battery. For this benchmark, we maintained our budget while accelerating physical construction of the custom sunshade mount.

By milestone three, we returned fully to schedule with a finalized design (V6) and a fully assembled umbrella housing ready for mounting. Budget goals were achieved and all technical specifications were satisfied.

V6 represented significant optimizations over V1. The solar tracking array was governed by four photoresistors arranged in a quadrant configuration separated by barrier walls. The system evaluated ambient light readings to calculate optimal positioning, actively rotating or tilting the panels toward direct sunlight whenever shadows fell across the directional sensors.

We also achieved major structural improvements. Across three development cycles, we achieved a 34% total weight reduction while centering the overall mass within the shade structure. Balancing the center of gravity within a constrained envelope prevented mechanical oscillation during active servo rotation, ensuring rotational stability and operational safety.

On commission day, our team completed testing five days ahead of schedule. We utilized this buffer to finalize our documentation and formal presentation, earning extra credit. NYU EG-1004 faculty selected the Solarbrella to be preserved as a permanent benchmark demonstration project for future engineering classes.

Oxford Hills Technical School hosts an annual Tech Challenge across programs to evaluate student skills through competitive design. The 2022 Pre-Engineering challenge specified creating an automated M&M dispenser engineered for operational consistency, accompanied by full engineering drawings and user manuals.

I engineered an automated architecture driven by an Arduino and servo actuators. The hardware consisted of a transparent upper reservoir feeding into four distinct channel tracks. Each channel guided individual pieces into a servo-actuated slide mechanism, routing pieces past a mechanical retention bar into a central collection funnel and exit nozzle.

To eliminate jamming risks, I implemented a pin-and-slot slider actuation geometry and programmed anti-jam timing routines. A brief delay-and-vibration cycle was coded into the sequence to align candies prior to slider actuation, accompanied by an LED indicator. An electronic torque limit safeguard was also integrated to automatically disengage the servo if an obstruction was encountered.

The completed project included full CAD documentation, featuring detailed manufacturing drawings for 18 custom components. The project earned 1st Place Gold in competition, leading to an invitation to serve as an official judge for the following year's challenge.

Developed as a core subsystem for the master Aquaponics project, this initiative focused on designing a low-maintenance, automated fish feeding mechanism using structured engineering design methodologies.

To combat humidity from water evaporation—a primary cause of food clogging in conventional feeders—we engineered a rotating disk transfer mechanism that isolates the main food storage vault from aquatic moisture. Portioned chambers in the disk index across isolated apertures to deliver consistent, dry portions without exposing bulk food to humidity.

I integrated solid-state transistor switching to replace clunky relays, adapting an ATX power supply to deliver stable power alongside an Arduino Uno control board. The entire mechanical housing was designed in CAD and 3D printed, featuring a top-mounted motor enclosure and an explicit power status indicator to confirm daily dispensing cycles.

The finished feeder provided reliable long-term autonomous feeding with minimal maintenance intervention.

Commissioned by the OHCHS Physics Department, this project called for designing and fabricating a precision laboratory slingshot to replace an aging wooden unit. The system required measurable force output and predictable angle selection for kinematics experiments.

Our design featured an extruded aluminum structural frame anchored by dual upright towers. The primary forward tower supported elastic cord mounts, while the rear tower housed a geared winch tensioning assembly and quick-release trigger. Central guide channels ensured linear pull alignment regardless of elevation angle settings.

I designed a custom flip-latch trigger mechanism incorporating an L-shaped holding arm, secondary mechanical safety catch, and lanyard release. The winch mechanism allowed operators to tension the elastic bands safely from behind the firing line while reading calibrated force scale indicators. The finished unit was assembled, calibrated, and delivered directly to the Physics Department for laboratory integration.
